Premier League predictor

A running project · Started September 2026
Machine learningFootball

I'm building a model that predicts the outcome of every Premier League game each week — win, draw, or loss, with a probability for each. I'm building it by hand, from scratch, as a way to learn: no ML libraries doing the modeling for me, just NumPy, SciPy, and the math.

The code is public.

What this is

Every week, the model outputs three probabilities for each of the ten Premier League fixtures: home win, draw, away win. The goal is simple to state and hard to do: beat the bookmakers' implied probabilities, week after week, measured honestly on games the model has never seen.

Bookmaker odds are the benchmark, never an input — the model doesn't get to peek at the answer key. Promoted teams stay in; every game gets a prediction, no exceptions.

How the model works

The core is a Dixon–Coles model, the classic statistical approach to football scores. Each team gets an attack rating and a defense rating, there's a league-wide home advantage, and a small correction for low-scoring games. From those ratings the model computes the probability of every scoreline from 0–0 to 10–10, then adds them up into home/draw/away probabilities.

The first version worked but had a flaw: with only a few games of data, newly promoted teams got extreme ratings. After four losses, Coventry's attack rating was so negative the model gave them a 0.04% chance at Nottingham Forest. They won. That one game blew up the week's score.

Version 2 fixes this two ways. First, shrinkage: a gentle prior that pulls ratings toward average until a team has played enough games to earn an extreme one — the same idea as not judging a batsman by his first over. Second, time decay: recent games count more than old ones, with a 180-day half-life. Both settings were tuned on two full past seasons the model was never evaluated on.

Everything is validated walk-forward: to predict a game, the model may only use games played before it. No leakage, no peeking. An Elo ratings system runs alongside as a standing challenger.

How I'll use it

Each week follows the same loop:

  1. Before the matchweek — publish the model's win/draw/loss probabilities for all ten games.
  2. After the matchweek — compare predictions against results, write up what the model got right and wrong, and add it as a new section below.
  3. Iterate — feed what I learn back into the model. v3 so far: uncertainty quantification done; Elo ensemble built (55/45 v2/Elo blend tuned on 2024–26 — honestly the gain over v2 alone is within noise). Next: expected-goals features, then injuries as a v4 parameter. The full-season bookmaker comparison waits for 380 games.

What I take from this week

  • 5/10, and ahead of Elo for the first time — on accuracy and log loss (1.065 vs 1.078). No bookmaker benchmark this week: the odds feed hadn't updated past matchweek 5, so there's nothing honest to report. Sixty games still say almost nothing; none of the gaps between the models are statistically meaningful yet.
  • Draw watch continues. Three draws, zero predicted. Draw prices sat between 20% and 32% all week — never the top pick, never far off. Long-run calibration still holds, but calibration over 760 games doesn't pay out in any given ten.
  • Strongest argument yet for xG features. Three of the five misses had the underlying numbers pointing the other way: Villa bottom of the xG-difference table vs third-place Brentford, Ipswich level with Fulham on xG with home advantage unpriced, and Newcastle's attack running on finishing variance (nine goals from 5.29 xG) with the league's leakiest underlying defense.
  • The United miss cuts the other way. xG agreed with the model — United rank fourth in xG difference — and the pick still lost. Persistent underperformance stops being luck and starts being finishing quality or goalkeeping at some point. Distinguishing that from noise is the harder problem, and it's on the list.
  • Coventry get their own note. Two weeks, two statement results for a promoted side with the league's lowest xG. Promoted teams are systematically weird for form-based models — no relevant history, high variance — which is why the excluded-promoted-teams ablation is still on the board.

What I take from this week

Four out of ten — level with the bookmakers on accuracy, a touch behind on log loss (1.144 vs 1.080), and behind the Elo challenger, which had the best week of the three at 5 of 10. An honest, average week.

A necessary caveat on all of the above, added after a review of this project: fifty games say almost nothing. The model's log-loss edge over the bookmakers across the five evaluated weeks is −0.015 per game with a standard error of 0.045 — statistically indistinguishable from zero. None of the gaps between the models clear that bar either. The honest reading is that the model is competitive with the bookmakers, and the ranking is unresolved until a full season of games accumulates.

The encouraging part: the two games where the model disagreed with the market, it was right both times — Brentford over Chelsea and Villa at Spurs. The draw story also got smaller on inspection: the evaluation window was unusually draw-heavy (32% vs ~26% long-run), and on 760 earlier games the model predicts draws at 25.1% against 25.9% actual — well calibrated. Draws are the hardest outcome for every model, but there's no structural blind spot to fix.

And the Forest–Coventry game did its job as a stress test. The old model's 0.04% was a failure of humility; 7% is a number a reasonable person could hold. Shrinkage works as advertised — though it's worth saying plainly that this one game accounts for nearly all of the measured v1-to-v2 improvement. The fix is principled; the victory lap is premature.
